## Product Description Statement

Around 50 words. Describes what the product will do to meet the users' goals.

## Problem Statement

The problem statement is the base of the Pre-design Stage. It's a concise description of an issue to be addressed or a condition to be improved upon.

1. IDEAL: describes the goals of the stakeholders.
2. REALITY: describes the current state of the process or product.
3. CONSEQUENCES: describes the impacts if the problem is not fixed or improved upon.
4. PROPOSAL: describe potential solutions.

## Stakeholders

Stakeholders refer to people interested in the product's outcome.

### Direct Users
Any user who will work with the product.

### Indirect Users
Individuals who do not directly interact with the product but are still affected by its use of benefit from its outputs. It also includes those who receive report from the product.

## Data Gathering

### User Interviews

These are exploratory, done in early stages of the development process to get a better understanding of potential users. Collect data about users' experience with an existing product. It's used in combination of user testing.

Interview techniques:
- Ask relevant questions to your product
- Consistency is important
- Avoid leading questions
- Avoid jargon
- Avoid long questions
- Need accurate records
- Need to make participants feel comfortable
- Written consent needed and sometimes ethics approval

####  Focus groups
A focus group encourages participants to discuss and interact with each other to generate deeper insights. It involves 3-10 users chosen as a representative sample of target population of interest. Requires a trained facilitator, who guides discussion, has a preset agenda - but unanticipated issues can be explored - and prompts participants.

Pros:
- enables people to put forward their opinions in a supportive social environment
- can be used in requirement gathering to identify conflicts in terminology or expectations between different users.


#### Running the interview
1. Introduction
2. Warm-up
3. Main body
4. Closure
#### Types of interview

##### Unstructured
- open-ended questions, process not pre-determined, can't replicate
- generate rich data, but more difficult to analyse

##### Structured
- closed questions, pre-determined, standardised procedure, replicable but may lack richness.
- used when study's goals are clear, need a pilot study.

##### Semi-structured
- both closed and open questions, start with pre-planned questions, then probe for more details.
- broadly replicable, provides a good balance between richness and ability to replicate.
#### Open vs closed questions

##### Open-ended
Questions that require more than a yes or no to respond. They require further explanation

##### Close-ended
Yes or no questions. Non-ambiguous answers.

#### Group Interviews
In a group interview, a researcher interviews multiple participants simultaneously to gather their individual perspectives on a topic.

Pros:
- Access to more participants in a shorter amount of time.
- Participants may feel less intimidated than in a one-on-one interview.
- Participants can build on each other's ideas.

Cons:
- Some participants may be shy about expressing their opinions.
- A skilled facilitator is needed to keep the discussion focused.
- Data analysis can be more challenging due to participant interactions and overlapping conversations.

### Questionnaires
These are similar to a structured interview. Can have closed and open questions. Can be distributed easily to a large number of people. It's less personal, so may be more difficult to get subject participation. Can be paper-based or online (email, web). The reliability of the answers is less than an interview.

##### Different ways of distributing questionnaires

Mail questionnaire:
- Good for highly personal topics
- Has to be completely self-explanatory
- No control over order survey is filled out
- Response bias
- Costly

Online questionnaire:
- Reach large numbers quickly
- Lower costs - no copying and postage
- Data analysis is quicker and easier
- Email - limited to text, but can target specific users
- Web-based - can include graphics, help-screens, pop-up screens and can enforce rules such as one selection only.

##### Steps involved in preparing a questionnaire
1. Decide what information is being sought
2. Decide on type of questionnaire to be used
3. Write a draft of the questionnaire
4. Re-examine and revise the questionnaire
5. Pre-test or pilot the questionnaire
6. Make changes and specify the procedure for use.

##### Questionnaire construction
- Order of questions is important
- Wording must be carefully chosen
- Think of the layout and pace
- Instructions clear and succinct

- Layout:
	- needs to be easy to understand and fill out
	- make aesthetically pleasing
	- include numbering
	- balance needed between using white space and keeping the questionnaire compact

- Provide __clear instructions__ on how to complete the questionnaire.
- Avoid long sentences - split compound sentences into two.
- Not too long overall.

### Observation

#### Direct Observation
In the field, and in controlled environments - think-aloud technique. Structuring frameworks are used to guide observation.

#### Indirect Observation
Tracking users' activities.
- Mouse movement distance and number of clicks
- Logs and web-analytics

#### Think Aloud
This is an observation technique, where users verbalise their thoughts continuously whilst interacting with a system. People don't naturally speak all the time, so you should encourage them. Facilitator prompts the participant if they have stopped verbalising ("what are you thinking about now?", "Describe what's on the screen"). The facilitator listens. You gather a lot of user comments about the interaction. Notes are taken, audio may be recorded if permission is given.

- This is a formal process involving participant consent
- You must explain the objective
- Give the participant a task scenario (describing a goal without implying specific tasks)
- Encourage to think aloud

#### Sample structuring framework to guide observation

[The Goetz and LeCompte (1984) framework](https://iier.org.au/iier14/smyth.html):
- Who is present?
- What is their role?
- What is happening?
- When does the activity occur?
- Where is it happening?
- Why is it happening?
- How is the activity organised?

#### Data Recording
- Notes, audio, video, photographs

### Key Issues when data gathering
1. Setting goals - decide how to analyse data once collected
2. Relationship with participants - clear and professional, with informed consent when appropriate
3. Multiple approaches - gather information/data using more than one approach
4. Pilot studies - small trial of main study

Data gathering is used both to develop requirements and for evaluation purposes.

## Analysis

You may gather both qualitative and quantitative data in your data collection. Then, you have to find patterns and use methods to group the data (consider affinity diagramming). Look for trends, consider statistics (if there are enough responses).

### Quantitative Analysis

Statistical analysis test such as: Mean, Standard deviation, T-test and ANOVA
Software: Excel, SPSS

### Qualitative Analysis
Finding patterns by categorising collected data using keywords: NVIVO
Using generative AI


### Data Visualisation

Why Visualise Data?
- helps interpret quantitative and qualitative findings
- reveals patterns, trends, and outliers
- supports design decisions
- communicates insights to stakeholders
- bridges data gathering and prototype design
